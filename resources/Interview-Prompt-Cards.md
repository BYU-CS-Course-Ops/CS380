# Interview Practice — Prompt Cards

*For the Session 4 mock-interview rounds. The "user" picks a real problem area they genuinely have (from the list, or their own) and answers truthfully. Interviewers do **not** pitch — they learn.*

---

## How to use (triads — 3 rounds)

- Roles: **Interviewer · User · Observer.** Rotate each round so everyone is Interviewer once.
- **User:** pick a card describing a problem area you *actually* experience; answer from your own life.
- **Interviewer:** use good, non-leading questions to understand the problem — no solutions, no pitching.
- **Observer:** tally leading questions and capture the real needs that surface.

## Problem-area cards (User: pick one you truly relate to)

1. **Commuting / parking / getting to class** — the daily logistics that go wrong.
2. **Group projects** — coordinating, dividing work, dealing with free-riders.
3. **Managing money as a student** — budgeting, splitting costs, tracking spending.
4. **Finding and protecting time** — study, work, church, social, sleep.
5. **A hobby or side interest** — the genuinely annoying part of doing it.
6. **A job or campus role** — a task that's more painful than it should be.
7. **Health / fitness routines** — starting, tracking, and sticking with them.
8. **Keeping up with a club / ward / community** — communication, events, coordination.
9. **Cooking / meals / groceries** — the recurring hassle.
10. **A tool or app you use** that frustrates you — where it falls short.

## Interviewer's funnel (works for any topic — you're starting cold)

The "user" picks the topic, so you won't know it in advance. Use this reusable flow to get from the surface to the *real* problem in about five minutes. Don't pitch — just follow it.

1. **Open the area (~30 sec):** "Tell me about [their topic] — how does that usually go for you?"
2. **Get a real, recent instance:** "When did it last go wrong or frustrate you? Walk me through it."
3. **Follow what they actually do:** "What do you do about it now?" — their **workaround** is where the hidden need lives.
4. **Ladder to the root — ask "why" 2–3 times:** "Why was that the hard part?" → "And why does *that* matter?"
5. **Measure the pain:** "How often does this happen? What does it cost you — time, money, stress?"
6. **Reflect it back:** "So it sounds like the real headache is ___ — is that right?" (This is how you surface a need they hadn't put into words.)
7. **If time:** "If you could wave a wand and fix one part of this, which would it be?"

## Reaching the issue they haven't named

People hand you a **symptom** ("group projects are stressful"), not the root problem. You reach the real, often-unspoken need by:

- **following the workaround** (a workaround is a pain they've quietly accepted),
- asking **"why / what happens then"** a couple of times to climb from symptom to stake, and
- **reflecting it back** in your own words until they say *"yeah — exactly."*

### Worked example (~5 exchanges)

*Topic the "user" picked: group projects.*

- **I:** "Tell me about group projects — how do they usually go?"
- **U:** "Honestly, stressful."
- **I:** "When did one last stress you out? Walk me through it."
- **U:** "Last week I ended up doing most of the slides the night before."
- **I:** "How did it get to that point?"
- **U:** "People said they'd do their parts and didn't — I found out too late."
- **I:** "What do you do about that now?" *(workaround)*
- **U:** "I just assume I'll have to redo things, so I over-prepare."
- **I:** "Why is finding out *late* the hard part?" *(why-ladder)*
- **U:** "Because by then there's no time to fix it without doing it myself."
- **I:** "So the real headache is you can't tell whether the work is actually happening until it's too late to react — is that it?" *(reflect back)*
- **U:** "Yeah, exactly. It's not the work — it's the not-knowing."

The surface complaint was "stressful group projects." The real, previously-unnamed need: **visibility into whether teammates are on track, early enough to act.** That's the kind of insight a good discovery interview surfaces — and you reached it in five questions, on a topic you knew nothing about going in.
