# The Mom Test — Key Ideas (Course Summary)

*A free, self-contained summary of the core ideas from Rob Fitzpatrick's* The Mom Test, *written for CS 301R so you don't have to buy anything. This is an original summary of the method — not a reproduction of the book. If you want the full treatment, the library has a physical copy.*

---

## The problem it solves

When you ask people about your idea, they lie — not maliciously, just to be nice. If you ask a friend *"would you use my app?"*, what they hear is *"please say yes so you don't hurt my feelings,"* and they oblige. So you walk away with **false validation**, feel great, and go build something nobody actually needs. **The Mom Test** is a set of habits that get you the truth instead. The name comes from the idea that your questions should be so grounded in facts that *even your mom* — who loves you and desperately wants to encourage you — couldn't accidentally mislead you.

## The three rules

**1. Talk about their life, not your idea.**
The moment you describe your idea, the conversation stops being about them and becomes about your solution — and they flip into "supportive friend" mode, reacting to what you built instead of telling you about their world. Keep your idea in your pocket. Ask how they *currently* handle the thing, what's annoying about it, and what they've already tried. You're a detective learning about someone's life, not a salesperson running a demo.

**2. Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or hypotheticals.**
People are terrible at predicting their own future behavior, and *"would you…?"* invites a cheerful, aspirational answer that means nothing. Past behavior is the only reliable evidence. Compare *"Do you exercise regularly?"* (almost everyone says "yeah, I try to") with *"Walk me through your workouts this past week"* (the truth comes out). Anchor every question to a real, specific, recent event.

**3. Talk less, listen more.**
Airtime is a fixed budget: every minute *you* spend talking is a minute you're not learning — and a minute you might spend accidentally steering them toward the answer you were hoping for. Aim to have them talking most of the time. Ask a short question, then get comfortable being quiet and letting them fill the space.

## What good questions dig for

- **Real, recent instances** — *"Walk me through the last time this happened."* A memory of a specific event is concrete and honest; a generalization ("I usually…") is a tidied-up reconstruction of how they *wish* things went. Specifics stop people from idealizing.
- **Current workarounds** — *"How do you deal with it today?"* If someone has already built a clumsy workaround, keeps a spreadsheet, or pays for a half-solution, the problem is real and it matters to them. If they do *nothing* about it, that's a strong hint it doesn't hurt enough to be worth solving.
- **The cost of the problem** — time, money, effort, or emotion. This tells you whether you've found a *painkiller* (worth paying to fix) or a *vitamin* (nice, but easily ignored). *"How much time does that cost you in a week?"* reveals far more than *"is it annoying?"*
- **Frequency and who's affected** — a painful problem that happens once a year may not be worth founding a project on; a mild one that happens *daily* to a *whole community* might be. Roughly: frequency × number of people ≈ the size of the opportunity.

## Signals to watch for

- **Compliments** ("cool idea!") — the most dangerous thing in the room, precisely because they *feel* like progress. They're just politeness, and they contain zero information about whether anyone will actually use your thing. Note them, then mentally throw them away.
- **Generics** ("I usually…", "I always…") — a summarized ideal, not a real event. Gently redirect: *"When was the last time? Walk me through it."*
- **Hypotheticals** ("I'd definitely use that," "I'd totally pay for it") — future promises are free to make and mean nothing. Pull back to the present: *"What are you using for this right now?"*
- **Real signals** — these are worth their weight in gold, because they cost the person something:
  - they already **spend time or money** on the problem,
  - they've **cobbled together a workaround** of their own,
  - they get **visibly frustrated or emotional** talking about it, or
  - they ask *you* to **follow up** ("let me know when it's ready," "can you email me?").

## Anti-patterns (leading yourself astray)

- **Pitching your idea, then asking "what do you think?"** — you'll get a review of your feelings, not the truth about their life.
- **Asking "would you use / buy X?"** — the classic leading question; it manufactures false yeses.
- **Fishing for compliments** — hunting for reassurance when you should be hunting for information.
- **Doing most of the talking** — if you leave a conversation having explained a lot, you learned a little.

## The one-line version

**Go learn about their life and their real, past behavior — and stay quiet long enough to hear it.**
