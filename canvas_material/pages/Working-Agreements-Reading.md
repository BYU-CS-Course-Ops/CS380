## The failure nobody plans for

Ask engineers about the worst team experience of their career and you will rarely hear about a hard technical problem. You will hear that someone went quiet for two weeks. That a decision got made in a hallway and nobody else found out. That one person kept doing everyone else's work and grew to resent it, silently, until they didn't.

None of those are failures of skill. They are failures of **unstated expectations**. One person believes replying within a day is obviously enough; another is annoyed by 9pm on Tuesday. One assumes real decisions need everybody; another assumes whoever owns the area just decides. Nobody is being unreasonable — they are running on different rules, and nobody wrote the rules down.

## The move: decide the rules before you need them

Groups that intend to last write down how they will work together. The artifact has different names in different places — a **working agreement**, a **team charter**, **rules of engagement**, a **contributing guide** — but the move is the same: make the expectations explicit **while everyone still likes each other**, because that is the cheapest possible moment to negotiate them.

The value is in the timing. Deciding "who breaks a tie?" while nothing is at stake takes four minutes of mild awkwardness. Deciding it during the tie means arguing about the rule and the issue at once, with a deadline overhead and people's pride involved. The same is true of every norm worth writing: the discussion is easy in advance and expensive in the moment.

A working agreement doesn't prevent conflict, and it isn't supposed to. It gives conflict somewhere to go.

## What earns a place in one

The test for every line is the same: **is it checkable, and would it survive a bad week?** "Communicate well" fails both. "Messages get a reply within 24 hours on weekdays" passes both.

Most working agreements cover some version of:

- **Who does what to start.** Not rigid job titles — lean responsibilities, plus the acknowledgment that they'll shift as the work reveals itself.
- **How we communicate.** Where the conversation happens, how fast a reply is expected, and what is urgent enough to interrupt someone's evening.
- **When we meet, and what a meeting owes us.** A meeting that produces no decisions and no assignments was a status email.
- **How decisions get made.** Which calls one person makes alone, which need everyone, and — the one teams skip — **who breaks a deadlock, and by what rule.**
- **What "pulling your weight" means here.** Stated concretely enough that someone could tell whether it happened: hours, visible contribution, and how the team raises it early when someone is slipping.

That last one carries the most weight and gets the least attention. A team that has never said out loud what happens the first time someone misses a commitment will handle it the way untrained groups always do: by saying nothing, twice, and then saying too much.

## Two things that make it real

**Everyone writes it.** An agreement drafted by one person is one person's assumptions with everyone else's names on it. The conversation *is* the artifact; the document is just where it's recorded.

**It's a living document.** Norms that reality disproves should be amended by agreement, not quietly ignored. A team that amends its agreement in week nine is working; a team whose agreement stopped describing them in week three and nobody said so is not.

**Working alone?** The same move applies with the audience changed. Your agreement is with yourself now — a cadence, a habit of recording decisions and why you made them, and a definition of "on track" you could check on a Friday. Later it becomes an agreement with strangers: the contribution guide and code of conduct that tell an arriving contributor how this project works are a working agreement written for people you haven't met yet.
