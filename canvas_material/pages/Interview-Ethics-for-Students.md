## Why this matters

When you interview someone for discovery, you're asking for their time, their attention, and their honesty — often about frustrations in their real life. Ethics here isn't red tape: people open up when they trust you, so treating them well *gets you better information*. And the founder you're becoming will do this for years. These are the norms we'll follow.

## Is this "research"? Do I need IRB approval?

**Short answer: no — not for this course.** BYU defines *research* as "a systematic investigation… designed to develop or contribute to **generalizable knowledge**" — knowledge meant to be published and to inform a whole field. Your discovery interviews are a **classroom project** meant to help *you* understand a problem and build a project — not to produce generalizable findings. BYU explicitly lists "classroom projects that are not expected to contribute to generalizable data" as **not requiring IRB review**.

Two important caveats:

1. **Not "research" does not mean "no ethics."** BYU still expects you to "follow ethical principles of your discipline as well as institutional policies and applicable laws," and student projects are overseen by the instructor. Everything below still applies.
2. **If the project changes, the answer can change.** If you later decide to *publish* your findings, or to systematically study a group to make broad, generalizable claims, that **would** be research and **would** need IRB approval *before* you collect more data. If you think you're crossing that line, talk to the instructor first.

*(See BYU IRB, "Is IRB Review Required?" — [irb.byu.edu/is-irb-approval-required](https://irb.byu.edu/is-irb-approval-required).)*

## The core principles

1. **Informed consent.** Before you start, tell people who you are, what you're doing, and why — then let them choose freely. No tricking, no pressure, no hidden agenda.
2. **Voluntariness — respect the "no."** They can decline, skip any question, or stop at any time, no explanation owed. Watch for *power imbalances* (interviewing people who might feel they can't say no to you) — they make a real "no" harder to give.
3. **Honesty.** You're there to learn, not to sell. Don't pretend it's an official study, don't oversell the project, and don't fish for a "yes."
4. **Privacy & confidentiality.** Collect only the identifying information you actually need. In your notes and your brief, **anonymize** — "a roommate," "a club officer," not full names. Never share what someone told you in a way that could identify or embarrass them.
5. **Recording.** Notes are fine. **Audio or video requires explicit permission**, asked before you begin. If they say no, don't.
6. **Minimize discomfort.** Some problems touch money, health, relationships, or failure. Let people share only what they're comfortable with; never push.
7. **Respect their time.** They're doing you a favor. Be on time, keep to the length you promised, thank them, and don't overstay.

## Extra caution: vulnerable people & sensitive topics

Some situations need more care — or a different problem space for a class project:

- **Minors (under 18):** get a parent/guardian's permission, and check with the instructor first.
- **Health, mental-health, legal, or financial-distress topics** (and anything that could cause real harm): clear it with the instructor before proceeding. Often a different, lower-stakes problem serves your learning just as well.
- **People who can't freely consent, or who might feel pressured** by their relationship to you.

**When in doubt, ask the instructor *before* you collect data — not after.** The habit generalizes: every organization that talks to users has someone who owns this question — a privacy or legal reviewer, a user-research lead, an ethics board — and checking with them before you collect is always cheaper than explaining afterward. Knowing *when to ask* is the professional skill; the instructor is just who you ask right now.

## A consent script you can actually use

> "Hi — I'm a student at BYU in a software engineering class, and I'm trying to understand how people deal with **[problem]**. Could I ask you a few questions? It'll take about ten minutes, there are no wrong answers, and you can skip anything or stop whenever you want. Is that okay? (And is it alright if I jot a few notes?)"

## The one-line version

**Be honest, ask permission, protect their privacy, respect their time and their "no" — and when a topic feels sensitive, check with the instructor first.**
