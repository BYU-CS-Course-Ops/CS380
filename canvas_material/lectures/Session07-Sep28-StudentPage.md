## Purpose

You've converged on an idea and defined it. Now you have to **argue for it** — in writing, to someone who hasn't lived inside your head for four weeks.

This is the move behind every funding request, design doc, RFC, and project pitch you'll ever write: a reader who owes you nothing has to finish your document believing the problem is real, the solution is sound, the scope is honest, and that *you* can actually build it. The hardest part is the one most people get wrong — **arguing feasibility without overpromising.** Enthusiasm reads as compensation for missing evidence. Every section either builds credibility or spends it, and once the balance goes negative, the reader stops believing your true claims too.

Your CP2 brief was written for you and your instructor. Your proposal is written to persuade a skeptic. *(In this course, that skeptic is concrete: a classmate deciding at Pitch Day whether to spend their semester on your project.)*

---

## Learning objectives

By the end of this session, you'll be able to:

1. **Name the sections of a software proposal** and say what each one must accomplish.
2. **Write for a skeptical reader** — apply the four checks (**audience · content · clarity · tone**) to your own draft.
3. **Argue feasibility honestly** — scope, risks, and mitigations, without overpromising.
4. **State non-goals**, and explain why scoping something *out* is as persuasive as scoping it in.
5. **Critique a proposal specifically enough for its author to act on** — the difference between "this is vague" and "your userbase is six audiences, so it's nobody."

## What we'll do together

A fast recall pass over the nine sections, then the two things reading can't give you. First, **the four checks** — audience, content, clarity, tone — with the live tests that go with them: hand your problem statement to someone who's never heard the idea and see if they can restate it in one sentence; read only your topic sentences, in order, and see whether they still form an argument. These four are the course's standing frame for technical writing, and you'll meet them again on bug reports, pull requests, design reviews, and your tech-comm portfolio.

Then the **dissection**. Two sample proposals argue for the *same project* — same idea, same student-level effort; the difference is discovery and cutting. You'll have read the weak one. We put its problem statement, its technical approach, and its non-goals on screen, find exactly where each loses its reader, and only then reveal how the strong version handled the same paragraph — including the difference between an *adjective* ("modern, industry-standard, flexible") and a *constraint* ("no IT staff on site, and uploads fail at this venue") as justification. We close on **feasibility** — the six risk categories, the know/learn/avoid triage, the spike, and the honest arithmetic on scope — then you'll outline your own proposal on a worksheet (provided) and trade it with a neighbor who knows nothing about your project.

## What you'll leave with

- **A one-line-per-section outline of your own proposal** — nine lines, each saying what *that section will claim*, plus your three biggest risks with dated or triggered mitigations, and a starred section marking where your evidence is thinnest. That star is your reviewer's first stop on Wednesday.
- **Specific feedback from a stranger** on your weakest section, your least-supported claim, and whether your schedule arithmetic is believable.
- **An honest read on your own scope.** Budget about **ten hours a week for the course in total** — class, reading, discovery, writing, and reviews all come out of it — and real building time runs for roughly **five weeks**. Anyone who sized their MVP by multiplying ten hours by fourteen weeks has already talked themselves into a project they can't finish.
