Proposal Review Protocol

Reviewing for   &

Work only its author has read is a guess. Review is how engineers find the flaw before it gets expensive — and it runs on the same three moves whether the artifact is a proposal, a design, or a pull request. This is the protocol we use all semester (design review Wk 6, code review Wk 7, repo audit Wk 11); today it runs on proposal drafts. CP3 due Mon, Oct 5.

As a reviewer

  1. Read silently, rubric in front of you, pen on the author's page. Start with scope realism and risks — where proposals fail — then problem & users.
  2. Mark three things in writing: strongest section and why · sharpest concern, in criteria language · one suggestion they could act on this week. Initial your comments.
  3. Discuss. A question lands better than a verdict: "what evidence supports this?" beats "this seems thin."

As an author

  • Capture, don't defend. You decide later what to act on — but you can't act on what you argued away.
  • Probe for the specific. "Which paragraph made you doubt it?" turns a worry into an edit.
  • Keep both marked-up copies. They are the evidence for Argument & revision, submitted with CP3.
  • Both readers come in cold — round 2 is an independent read, and adds one question: "Would this pitch recruit you?"

1 · The register — say it so it can be used

Usable — specific, criteria-anchoredNot usable — taste, or a shrug
"Scope realism — I don't believe the calendar sync fits five weeks of real building." "This seems like a lot."
"Problem & users — 'students' isn't bounded. Which ones, how many, how did you reach them?" "The problem section is vague."
"Risks — these could be in anyone's proposal. Which one is specific to this stack?" "Good risks section."

2 · What you are reading for — the CP3 rubric

The two criteria marked ★ are where proposals fail — start your read there, then work the rest. Name the criterion when you raise a concern; that is what makes feedback actionable instead of personal.

CriterionWtStrong looks like
Problem & user clarity15A compelling, evidence-backed problem and a bounded userbase — not "students" or "everyone."
Solution & core features15A coherent solution, a sharp ranked feature set, and explicit non-goals.
Feasibility & approach20Stack and dependencies actually researched; the approach is justified, not asserted.
★ Scope realism (MVP)15A vertical slice sized against the real build window — roughly five weeks — with the arithmetic shown.
Richness & extensibility15Exercises several engineering dimensions; a credible path for others to build on it.
★ Risks & mitigation10Real, project-specific risks with mitigations that carry a date or a trigger.
Argument & revision10Persuasive writing with visible response to peer feedback — measured against today's draft.

Argument & revision requires a draft on file. Today's LMS upload is what your revision is measured against; with no draft on file the criterion caps at Developing.

3 · If the finding is "too big" — the narrowing moves

  • Cut the platform, keep the slice — one user type, one workflow, end to end.
  • Demote features to non-goals — naming what you are not building strengthens the proposal.
  • Shrink the user — one club, one ward, one lab, one theater.
  • Defer the hard integration — v1 proves the core; the sync is the roadmap's job.
Then check the arithmetic out loud. The ~10 hrs/week is everything — class, reading, discovery, writing, reviews. Subtract those first, then multiply what's left by roughly five weeks of real building. Most overscoped proposals die on this line.
