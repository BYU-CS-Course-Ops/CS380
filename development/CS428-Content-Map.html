What would be lost

Ranked by how much each loss matters to the capstone.

  1. Large-team dynamics. Brooks's law, communication overhead, conceptual integrity, and Conway's law. The capstone puts 50 students on 13 teams, and its org structure is built on these ideas. Students will experience every one of these effects, but no course in the sequence, and none of CS 240, 340, or 329, names them or gives a way to reason about them. This is the highest-value loss, and the cheapest to fix.
  2. Estimation and scheduling. Today's CS 380 teaches a light version (relative sizing and a fit check). Neither v2 nor the program outline covers estimation, scheduling, or the critical path. CS 480 asks teams to "plan iterations" without teaching how.
  3. Honest status reporting and metrics. The Thermocline of Truth, metric gaming, and reporting bad news upward early. The capstone has mentoring logs and release-readiness reports, and CS 329 teaches system metrics and customer status pages. None of them teaches reporting project status honestly.
  4. The Peopleware material. The work environment, hiring and retention, quality under schedule pressure, and jelled teams and teamicide. Teamicide matters most for v2, which reshuffles teams every three weeks.
  5. Organizational politics and judgment about technology hype. Webster's political and conceptual pitfalls, organization types, and adopting a technology for the wrong reason.
  6. Legal practice beyond open-source licensing. Patents, trade secrets, NDAs, non-competes, ownership of side projects, the CFAA, and litigation and discovery. v2 adds privacy and compliance law (FERPA, HIPAA, GDPR) but none of this.
  7. The ACM Code of Ethics as the named professional standard. Ethics is taught throughout the sequence, grounded in the BYU Aims and the IRB, but never in the profession's own code.
  8. The failure-case literature as evidence: why software projects fail, runaway projects, IT litigation patterns. With it goes the citation-based case exam that makes students apply that literature.
  9. A comparison of process models. CS 329 tells the history from Agile to DevOps and SRE, and the studio has students live one process. Nobody asks them to weigh one model against another.

Covered as well or better elsewhere: requirements, architecture and design, organizational roles, risk, scope control, code review, configuration management, architecture and design (CS 340), test planning and testing fundamentals (CS 329, CS 240, CS 340), coding style and refactoring (CS 240), post-mortems (CS 329 incident reports), maintenance (CS 481), retrospectives, and the experience of building and demonstrating a product in public.

Where the lost material could go

Losses 1–5 amount to roughly four or five reading-and-discussion sessions; 6–9 are one session or less each.

CS 380 v2

Research, constraints, architecture, and probes (Weeks 4–5, 8B, 9A, 11A)

  • Armour's orders of ignorance, as the frame for research
  • Throwaway prototypes, "plan to throw one away" (11A Probe Technology)
  • Estimation fallacies (9A Weigh Tradeoffs)
  • Judgment about technology hype (8B Explore Architecture)
  • IP beyond licensing, and employment agreements (5A Find Constraints, 5B license considerations)
  • The ACM Code, alongside the consent and ethics work in 2A

CS 480

Weeks 1–3, "establish the engineering system"

  • Brooks's law, communication overhead, and Conway's law, taught just as the 13 teams form
  • Estimation and scheduling, with the critical path applied to integration milestones
  • Status reporting and the Thermocline of Truth

CS 481

Weeks 12–15, maintainership and stewardship

  • The Peopleware material: environment, retention, jelled teams, leadership
  • Organizational politics
  • Technology lifecycles and platform obsolescence

CS 340 (Software Design)

Alongside Clean Architecture and the design principles

  • No Silver Bullet: essential vs. accidental complexity
  • Conceptual integrity, and the second-system effect
  • Meier & Rechtin's heuristics for systems architecting, next to the "Ten Design Commandments"

CS 329 (QA and DevOps)

Extending lessons it already has

  • Formal inspections, beside its testing-categories lesson
  • Project metrics and how they get gamed (Webster's "Lies, Damned Lies, and Project Metrics"), extending its metrics unit from system health to project progress
  • The Thermocline of Truth, extending its status-reporting lesson from customers to managers
  • A comparison of process models, extending its history lesson

CS 452 (Database)

A weak fit. Records preservation for litigation and discovery could sit with its data-retention material. Nothing else from CS 428 maps naturally.

CS 240

Already covers coding style, refactoring, unit testing, code coverage, and git. Nothing new is needed.

No natural home

  • The failure-case literature as a body of evidence, and the case exam that tests it. It could become a reading thread that runs through CS 380–481.
  • Unauthorized access (the CFAA). It would fit a security unit if one is added.

What CS 428 is

CS 428 is a 3-credit course that meets once a week. It has three strands:

A reading canon, assessed by exam. The Mythical Man-Month, Peopleware, and Glass's Facts and Fallacies of Software Engineering; Armour's "Five Orders of Ignorance"; Meier & Rechtin's "Heuristics for Systems Architecting"; and about 20 of Bruce Webster's essays. Readings and podcasts are 30% of the grade. A 3-hour, open-book, case-based midterm requires students to cite those readings; it is 25% of the grade, and students must score at least 60% on it to pass the course.

Teams run as startup companies. Each team has an org chart with a project manager and a chief architect, and keeps a public wiki. Deliverables are requirements, PERT and Gantt charts, an architecture and design document, a test plan, and prototype, work-in-progress, and final demos (27%).

Weekly status reports with billable hours (10%), plus an individual code review and a post-mortem.
