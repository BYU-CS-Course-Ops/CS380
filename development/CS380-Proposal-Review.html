CS 380 Proposal Review

Tom Stephens, BYU Computer Science · October 17, 2026

If the CS 380 proposal were adopted, about a quarter of its 30 class meetings could reuse material from the current pilot, CS 301R, with light revision. Most of the rest could adapt 301R material, and four would be written from scratch. Building and open-source work would move to CS 480 and CS 481. Eleven 301R practices that the proposal doesn't mention are cheap to keep.

How to read this. The CS 380 page is one proposal, not a settled plan; everything here describes what adopting it would imply. Where it differs from the same author's earlier SE Studio Program Outline, the newer CS 380 page wins. The outline is used only for what the CS 380 page doesn't describe: CS 480, CS 481, and the support courses that run alongside CS 480 (CS 329, CS 452, CS 348).

301R practices named below

Evidence tags
Every claim in a brief or persona is marked [E] observed or [A] assumed, so readers can see what rests on evidence.
Ethics gate
A hands-up check that closes the interviewing workshop: consent, the BYU IRB boundary for class projects, and power imbalance. Nobody interviews before passing it.
Spike
A short, time-boxed experiment that answers one feasibility question. A spike that fails (“red”) still counts as a success. Every proposal must include one.
Review protocol
A printed peer-review sheet: the strongest section, the sharpest concern in rubric language, and one suggestion the author can act on that week.
Scope arithmetic
Narrowing moves (one user, one workflow end to end; demote features to non-goals) plus an estimate against the hours actually available.
Paired samples
A strong and a weak proposal for the same invented project, dissected side by side in class.
Cold read
A partner reads the project's README and contributing guide without help and records where they got stuck.
Team charter
A one-page working agreement, written in class the day teams form.
Prop Loft
A sample open-source project built ahead of the class; tagged snapshots serve as the worked example for git, issues, and project boards.
Handout convention
Every assignment opens with why it exists (the skill, where it fits, its use after the course) and teaches the general principle before the course-specific version.

Both put evidence before solutions, put real users first, ground ethics in the Aims of a BYU Education, and grade reasoning over polish. The proposal’s learning outcomes closely match the first four of 301R’s eight. The other four (open-source founding, git workflow, technical communication, and building and launching) aren’t in it.

What leaves CS 380, and where it goes

Most of what leaves 301R has a home later in the sequence. Building moves to CS 480 (6 credits), and open source is split across CS 480, CS 481, and the cross-cutting teams with no single owner. Two topics 301R teaches have no home at all. Later courses are as sketched in the program outline: CS 380 (fall) → CS 480 (winter) → CS 481 (the next fall).

What CS 380 would need to add

Twenty additions in three groups. The operations group is the one with no 301R precedent at all.

Content

  1. Regulatory and compliance constraints (5A): a reading, a “questions for a qualified reviewer” worksheet, and the not-certification guardrail.
  2. UX design (7B) and usability testing (10B): journeys, task flows, low-fidelity prototyping, accessibility basics, a think-aloud protocol.
  3. Requirements with quality attributes (8A): a scenario template.
  4. Architecture options at enterprise scale (8B): hosting, distribution, deployment, cost and elasticity; a comparison matrix.
  5. Data protection and threat modeling (11B): data classification, retention, a threat-model worksheet.
  6. Decision records and traceability (14B): an ADR template and a requirement → decision → evidence matrix.
  7. Quality planning (14A): test strategy, accessibility, deployment, and operations plan templates.
  8. Coordination across teams (13B): team design, interface contracts, cross-team syncs, leadership roles.
  9. Research methods (4A–4B): a research-question template, a source and method log, a synthesis technique.
  10. Stakeholder mapping (1B), and observation as a research method (2A).

Process and structure

  1. A repeated block template: six meetings, fixed deliverables, and one handout pattern used five times.
  2. Gallery-rotation reviews and a faculty selection protocol: published criteria, a documented rationale, and the mechanics for merging and redirecting teams.
  3. Five reflection prompts, and the strength / question / next step form generalized from 301R's review protocol.
  4. A revise-before-scoring workflow in GitHub: a docs folder per stage, pull-request submission, and a revision window each block.
  5. Five rubrics on the 0–4 scale (the proposal already drafts them).
  6. Handling team churn: rules for carrying research forward, credit for merged teams' work, and morale framing.

Operations (no 301R precedent at all)

  1. An external partner program: recruitment, participation agreements, a role at each review, and a fallback when partners withdraw.
  2. Grading calibration across five faculty: norming sessions and shared exemplars.
  3. Mentor training: a role description, a coaching guide, and a check-in form.
  4. Room logistics: capstone room and animation theater bookings.

301R practices worth keeping

These eleven aren’t in the proposal, but each is cheap to keep, and most address a risk the proposal itself names.

Block 2 or 3

A required early spike

The proposal first tests technical assumptions in week 11, after the design is selected in week 9. One small spike per team before the design review keeps selection from resting on untested feasibility, which is the proposal's own “technical uncertainty is deferred” risk.

From 301R: Spikes reading; proposal feasibility criterion

End of 2A

An ethics gate before talking to real people

The proposal teaches consent but never checks it. With 50 students and external partners, a hands-up gate is the cheapest protection the course has.

From 301R: Interviewing workshop; BYU IRB-grounded ethics guide

Every brief

Evidence tags

The rubrics ask teams to separate facts from assumptions but give them no way to do it. Tagging is that way, and it works across all five checkpoints.

From 301R: Persona template and examples

Weeks 1–2, feeding 2B

Individual idea generation before teams

Teams of four on day one tend to anchor on the first or loudest idea. A week of individual divergent work protects the divergent half of each cycle.

From 301R: Idea journal; problem-space workshop

9A and 15A

Honest scope arithmetic

The proposal names “enterprise scope overwhelms CS 380 or CS 480” but gives no method. 301R's estimate against real hours transfers to CS 480's capacity: students × credit hours × build weeks.

From 301R: Narrowing moves; feasibility and risk worksheet

13A–14B

Appropriate detail vs. over-specification

CS 380 writes a design that CS 480 and three support courses will build. Over-specify it and they're locked in; under-specify it and they're stranded.

From 301R: Design-document session and template

15A

A cold-read test of the handoff

After the week 12 merge, most CS 480 students won't have written the design they receive. A partner team reading it cold (“could you start work Monday?”) tests readiness better than a presentation does.

From 301R: Cold-read sheet

1A, then each block's first meeting

A team charter, re-signed at each merge

The proposal says to agree on team norms but gives no instrument. Teams reform up to four times; a one-page charter is taught once and reused.

From 301R: Team charter; working-agreements reading

Block 5

The license decision

The program makes open source a goal, but no course owns the license choice, and code starts in CS 480's first week.

From 301R: License cheat sheet; licensing session

Each block's practice meeting

Paired strong and weak samples

Dissecting a strong and a weak version of the same brief shows what each rubric level looks like, and it scales to 50 students.

From 301R: Paired proposals; Prop Loft samples

All handouts

Handouts that explain themselves

With rotating faculty and five checkpoints, each handout should carry its own rationale.

From 301R: 301R handout convention

Smaller items worth offering: pass conditions (every major artifact at Developing or better, so a strong showcase can’t hide a missing research brief); a draft-on-file rule (no draft, no revision credit, which keeps revise-before-scoring honest); and one reading check, only where a session’s timing depends on the reading.

Questions for the committee

The most pressing is who owns open source, and when the license gets chosen.

  1. Open source needs an owner and a date. The program outline makes it a goal (“open social software,” contribution guides, maintainership plans). But no course chooses the license, and contributor pathways arrive only at the end of CS 481. Suggestion: settle the license and repository governance in CS 380's final block, before support courses write code.
  2. Two expectations from the earlier outline aren't clearly carried forward. The outline put technical proofs of concept in weeks 4–7; the CS 380 page moves them to week 11, after design selection. The outline also expected CS 380 to hand off the full 13-team model, team charters, and repository, hosting, and development-environment requirements. The CS 380 page's final block covers teams and leads, but not charters or infrastructure requirements. The committee should confirm whether both changes are intended.
  3. The handoff may carry more weight than the CS 380 page suggests. If the support courses go ahead, CS 329, CS 452, and CS 348 treat the CS 380 proposal as their “source of truth.” That raises the bar on CS 380's design document and backlog.
  4. Ownership and motivation. 301R runs on every student owning a project. The proposal merges most students' framings three or four times before week 12. Its mitigations are sound, but the motivation cost is real, and 301R's Pitch Day design is one model for handling it.
  5. Five selections in 15 weeks is a lot of convergence. 301R spends weeks 3–5 building up to one. The idea-scoring method should run at every block, not just the first.
  6. The calendar won't hold 30 meetings. Fall 2026 had 27 (a Wednesday start, Labor Day, Thanksgiving). The block template needs a rule for which meeting gives way; the finals slot can host the showcase.
  7. AI policy. “Use AI to accelerate results” sits against 301R's “a tool, not the topic; defend everything.” Individual grades in the proposal rest on reflections, which AI writes easily.
  8. Individual accountability at 50 students. One rubric row per checkpoint plus reflections is thin evidence against free-riding in teams that keep reshuffling. 301R's peer-evaluation design was built for exactly this.
  9. Workload. 2.5 hours in class plus 6 outside is about 8.5 hours a week, a little under the usual 9 for 3 credits. The per-block deliverable list is heavy for 3 hours of individual time.

Topics that carry over

19adapt it
4are new

The reuse meetings are 2A, 9A, 10A, 12A, 13A, 15A, and 15B; the new ones are 5A (compliance), 7B (UX), 11B (data protection), and 14B (decision records). Two of the adapt meetings, 10B and 13B, are close to new. About 17 of 301R’s 27 sessions contribute substantial material. Whatever design the committee chooses, these 301R topics belong in CS 380:

Finding and validating a need
Interviewing real people, avoiding leading questions, consent and research ethics, and personas that separate evidence from assumption.
Framing a problem
Users, prioritized needs, goals, non-goals, and success criteria, written down before any solution.
Evaluating ideas against explicit criteria
Scoring candidates, weighing value against effort, and committing with a written rationale.
Feasibility and risk
Risk categories, de-risking the top one or two, and spikes that test one question in a fixed time.
Scoping honestly
Narrowing to what must be proved first, naming non-goals, and estimating against the hours actually available.
Writing and defending a proposal
Arguing from evidence without overpromising, and pitching to a critical audience.
Design documents and architecture
System context, components, data model, and decisions with the alternatives considered, at the right level of detail.
Structured peer review
Feedback anchored in criteria, given and received without defensiveness, then turned into ranked revisions.
Teamwork
Charters and working agreements, written when a team forms.
Planning the work
Breaking a design into epics, milestones, and a prioritized backlog someone else can start on.
Reporting results honestly
Separating what was observed from what is claimed, and stating limitations plainly.
