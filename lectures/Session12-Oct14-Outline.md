## Purpose of this session

Monday you learned to read a design doc; today your team **produces the core of yours** — live, on whiteboards, with the instructor circulating as design reviewer. Diagramming forces the conversations teams otherwise defer: where does state live? what talks to what? what happens when the external API is down? By the end of class every team has a system-context diagram, a component/data-flow diagram, a first-cut data model, and their two biggest decisions argued with alternatives — which is to say, the hard 80% of the design-doc draft.

The session leans hard on prior coursework, and the instructor's job is to keep saying so. **Most of today's moves are ones they already own from CS 240 and CS 340 — the altitude is what's new.** Both of those courses handed students an architecture and asked them to model or build it; this is the first time they define the boundary, choose the components, and defend the choice. Naming that difference out loud is most of the teaching.

---

## Learning objectives

By the end of this session, a student can:

1. **Draw a system-context diagram** — the system, its users, and its external dependencies. *(New — neither prior course draws the boundary.)*
2. **Decompose the system into components** with clear responsibilities and interfaces. *(Re-aimed — they know SRP and layering; the altitude and the freedom to choose are new.)*
3. **Trace a core user action end-to-end** through the components (data flow). *(Known — CS 240 Phase 2 did this seven times; choosing *which* flow is new.)*
4. **Sketch the data model** — entities, key fields, relationships. *(Known — CS 240 ERDs; the MVP/roadmap scope cut is new.)*
5. **Argue one design decision against a real alternative** in writing. *(New — both prior courses specified the stack.)*

## Timed outline (≈75 min)

### 1. Framing + the worked example — 8 min

- Today produces the draft. The sequence: **context → components → a traced flow → data → decisions.** Show all four diagrams for the worked example fast — the target is *this*, at whiteboard fidelity.
- **Name the fidelity explicitly:** boxes, arrows, labels, marker. Not a diagramming tool, not UML syntax, not the polished artifact they exported from sequencediagram.org for CS 240 Phase 2. *Nothing today gets typed up until you photograph it.*
- **Name the altitude explicitly** (this is the framing that prevents half the problems below): these are **system-level** diagrams — services, stores, components, boundaries. CS 240 and CS 340 mostly had them drawing **class-level** diagrams — objects, methods, lifelines. Same notation instincts, one level up. If a box on their board has a method list, they've dropped an altitude.
- Rule for the room: **every box must have a job; every arrow must carry something nameable.** If you can't label the arrow, you've found a design question — good, that's the point.
- Say where the rounds land: each one fills a numbered section of the template, in order. By 3:00 today they will have drafted §3, §4, §5 and §6 without writing a document.

> **Instructor notes — §1**
> **Slide:** the four-diagram sequence, the Backstage worked example, the two rules (every box has a job; every arrow carries something nameable), and the round→template map.
> **The one framing sentence to land:** *"You've drawn all of these before. You've never chosen what goes in them."*
> **If the room is quiet at the start:** ask one question — *"In CS 240, who decided that chess needed Handlers, Services and DAOs?"* The answer is "the assignment did." That's the whole setup.

### 2. Round 1 — system context — 10 min

- Teams draw: the system as one box; every human role and external service around it; arrows labeled with what crosses the boundary.
- Forcing questions: who are *all* the user types (from CP2)? what external services do you depend on (the dependency risks from Week 3)? what does each boundary arrow actually carry?

> **Instructor notes — §2 (Round 1: system context)**
>
> **Status: this is the new one.** Budget your attention here. It looks trivial and it isn't, because nothing in CS 240 or CS 340 ever asked them where their system ends.
>
> **Key definitions (put these on the slide):**
> - **System boundary** — everything you will build, own, and can change. One box. If you'd have to file a bug with someone else to fix it, it's outside.
> - **Actor / user role** — a *kind* of person who interacts with the system, named by their job, not their name. (Backstage: costume manager, borrower, visiting director.)
> - **External dependency** — any system you don't control that yours needs: a third-party API, an auth provider, a payment processor, email/SMS, a hosting platform, a package registry, GitHub itself.
> - **Boundary arrow** — one direction, labeled with the *noun that crosses*: "photo upload," "OAuth token," "overdue reminder," not "communicates with."
>
> **Remind them:** the CS 240 chess top-level diagram (Client · Server · Database) *was* a context diagram — drawn for them. Ask what was outside the box there, and what would have gone outside if chess had used a real auth provider.
>
> **Teach:** the boundary is a *decision*, not an observation. Putting something inside means you own it, test it, and fix it at 11pm. Putting it outside means you inherit its downtime. Round 3's failure case comes straight from what they put outside.
>
> **Common errors and the correction:**
> - **Drawing internals.** Half the room will open the box immediately. "That's the next round — close the box." If they've already got components, it isn't wasted; park it.
> - **Tech stack instead of actors.** "React," "Postgres," "Flask" are not around the box; they're inside it or absent. Around the box are *people and systems you don't own*.
> - **Only the happy user.** Push for the roles nobody lists: the admin, the person who does initial setup, the moderator, the *maintainer* — and, in this course, the **future contributor**, who is a user of the repo even if not of the app.
> - **Forgetting the boring externals.** Email delivery, the hosting platform, the identity provider, the app store. These are exactly the ones that cause outages.
>
> **If a team is stuck, say:**
> - *"Name one person who touches this system who isn't your main user."*
> - *"If this service vanished tomorrow, would you have to change code? Then it goes outside the box."*
> - *"Read me one arrow out loud as a sentence: 'the borrower sends ___ to the system.'"*
>
> **What "done" looks like:** one box, three to six things around it, every arrow labeled with a noun, and no component names inside the box.

### 3. Round 2 — components & interfaces — 15 min

- Open the box: 3–6 components, each with a one-line responsibility. Draw the arrows between them; name each interface roughly (REST? function calls? a queue?).
- Watch-fors the instructor probes while circulating: the **God component** (one box doing everything — split it), the **mystery arrow** (unnameable data), the **missing state** (where does data live? — if no box owns it, that's a gap).

> **Instructor notes — §3 (Round 2: components & interfaces)**
>
> **Status: mostly re-aiming.** They have the concepts; they've never had the choice.
>
> **Key definitions (slide):**
> - **Component** — a unit with one responsibility, its own interface, and a name you could put on a contributor's first task. Roughly: something you could hand to one person for two weeks. **A component is not a class**; a component *contains* classes.
> - **Responsibility** — one sentence, one reason to change. (SRP, from CS 240/340, applied one level up.)
> - **Interface** — *how* two components talk, named roughly: an in-process function call, HTTP + JSON, a WebSocket, a queue, a scheduled job, a file drop. They have all of this vocabulary already.
> - **State owner** — the component that is allowed to write a given piece of data. Exactly one.
>
> **Remind them (name the courses):** CS 240's chess stack — **Server → Handlers → Services → DataAccess → Database** — is a perfectly respectable default architecture, and most teams here should end up with something close to it. CS 340's Clean Architecture is the same idea with the dependency rule made explicit. **Say the useful part:** that layering was a choice someone else made, and it was a good one for that problem. Their job is to make it deliberately.
>
> **Teach:**
> - **Altitude.** 3–6 boxes. If they're at fifteen, they've slipped into a CS 340 class diagram; ask them to group. If they're at two ("frontend" and "backend"), they haven't started; ask what the backend *does*, in verbs.
> - **Layers vs. tiers**, if it comes up — CS 240 taught it: layers are logical grouping, tiers are physical distribution. A team that wants "microservices" for a five-week build is usually describing layers.
> - **Interfaces are where teams split.** Two people can work in parallel exactly as far as their interface is agreed. Worth saying to teams of three or four; worth saying to solo students too, because a contributor arrives at an interface.
>
> **Common errors and the correction:**
> - **Layer names as components.** "Frontend / Backend / Database" is three boxes that say nothing. Push: *what does the backend do?* Split on the verbs.
> - **The God component** (in the outline above) — usually named after the project itself. If the box is called "Backstage," it isn't a component.
> - **Class-diagram drift** — boxes with method lists, 20+ of them. "Wrong altitude, right instinct — group those into four."
> - **Unowned state.** Ask "who writes this?" of every data item in the room.
> - **Pattern-dropping.** Someone will propose a CS 340 pattern by name (observer, strategy, facade) as if naming it settles something. Fine answer: *"Good — what problem in your system does it solve?"* If there's no answer, it goes in the parking lot.
>
> **If a team is stuck, say:**
> - *"Say what your system does in one sentence. Now break that sentence at every 'and.' Those are your components."*
> - *"What's the first self-contained piece of work you'd hand a new contributor? That's a component."*
> - *"Point at the box that owns the data. If your finger is between two boxes, that's the design question."*
>
> **What "done" looks like:** 3–6 boxes, a one-line responsibility written *on* each, every arrow labeled, and exactly one box owning each kind of data.

### 4. Round 3 — trace one core flow — 12 min

- Take the **single most important user action** (the heart of the MVP — e.g., "student reserves a room") and trace it end to end: user → UI → logic → data → back. Number the steps on the diagram.
- If the trace hits a component your diagram doesn't have, add it. If it skips half your boxes, ask whether those boxes are MVP or roadmap.
- Fast follow: trace the **failure case** (the external API is down; the reservation conflicts) — one minute, arrows in red. Error handling is a cross-cutting concern; this is where it becomes visible.

> **Instructor notes — §4 (Round 3: trace one core flow)**
>
> **Status: they own this move.** Don't teach it. **Say the sentence and get out of the way:** *"You did this seven times in CS 240 — one sequence diagram per chess endpoint, happy path plus errors. Same move, one flow, no tool."* That's the entire instruction, and it will save you six minutes.
>
> **Key definitions (slide):**
> - **Core flow** — the one user action that, if it didn't work, would make the product pointless. Not the most complex one; the *load-bearing* one.
> - **Trace** — numbered steps across the component diagram, starting at the user's finger and ending at what the user sees.
> - **Failure case** — the same trace when something outside your control breaks.
>
> **Teach (the part that's actually new): choosing the flow.** In CS 240 the seven endpoints were handed to them. Here, picking is the work, and the pick has consequences all semester: **the core flow is the vertical slice, which is milestone 1 in §8 of the template, which is what they demo in November.** Say that link out loud — it's the most valuable sentence in the round.
> - The test: *"If this one action worked and nothing else did, would anyone use it?"* If no, it's not the core flow.
> - Watch for teams tracing the *admin* flow or the *signup* flow. Signup is never the core flow.
>
> **On the failure case — borrow their CS 240 vocabulary and then go past it.** They handled error paths as status codes (401, 403, 500). At this altitude the interesting failures are the ones their code didn't cause: **the third party is down, the network drops mid-action, two people act at once.** CS 240's distributed-architectures module gives you three words for this, worth saying in this order:
>   1. **Partial failure** — one piece is down while the rest keeps running. That is the shape of every failure at this altitude.
>   2. **“The network is reliable”** — the first of the Fallacies of Distributed Computing, and *exactly* the planted defect in Monday's sample: the traced flow assumed every request succeeds. Naming the fallacy makes the defect land as a known failure mode rather than as sloppiness.
>   3. **Idempotency** — CS 240's own solutions list puts it as *“performing the same operation multiple times has the same effect as performing it once.”* This is the one that pays off immediately, because it *is* the retry problem in the red trace: if the volunteer taps Check out twice, or the retry duplicates, you must not write two checkout records — and **“one open checkout per item” on the worked-example component diagram is the guard that makes the retry safe.** Point at the diagram and close the loop.
>
> **Don't reach for CAP here**, even though it sits on the same CS 240 page. CAP governs replicas of a data store disagreeing during a partition; Backstage has one database and no replication, and a phone with no signal is a client that can't reach the server, not a partition between nodes. A sharp student can push back on that, and you would be defending a stretch.
> - Three prompts that produce real failure cases: *What if it's slow instead of down? What if the write half-succeeded? What if two users do this in the same second?*
> - The user-visible half matters as much as the system half: **what does the person see, and what can they do next?** "Show an error" is not an answer; "the form keeps what they typed and retries" is.
>
> **Common errors and the correction:**
> - **Tracing at class altitude** — lifelines for objects instead of components. Redirect once; it's muscle memory.
> - **A trace that touches two boxes.** Either the flow is too small or the component diagram is decorative. Usually the latter — good find, fix Round 2.
> - **A trace that needs a box that isn't there.** Add it. This is the round doing its job.
> - **Skipping the failure case** because the minute is tight. Don't let it go; it's a graded CP5 criterion (Risk & open questions) and the single most common gap in Monday's sample.
>
> **If a team is stuck, say:**
> - *"Start at the user's finger. What do they tap, and what do they see one second later?"*
> - *"Where in this trace does something get written down? What happens if that write fails halfway?"*
> - *"Which of the things outside your box in Round 1 is in this trace? Turn it off and trace it again."*
>
> **What "done" looks like:** numbered steps over the component diagram, start and end at the user, and a red-pen variant with a named user-visible outcome.

### 5. Round 4 — data model first cut — 10 min

- Entities, key fields, relationships — boxes and labelled lines, whiteboard fidelity. Check it against the traced flow: every step that reads or writes data must land on an entity.
- Scope check: entities that only exist for roadmap features get a dashed border — in the doc's "future" note, not the MVP schema.

> **Instructor notes — §5 (Round 4: data model first cut)**
>
> **Status: recall, not instruction.** CS 240's relational-model unit covered entities, primary keys, foreign keys, 1:1 / 1:N / M:N, what makes a good key, and ERDs outright. **Say so and start the timer:** *"Crow's feet, keys, the join table — CS 240. Ten minutes, whiteboard fidelity."*
>
> **Key definitions (slide — one line each, as reminders only):**
> - **Entity** — a thing the system has to remember. Usually a noun the user would recognize.
> - **Key fields** — the three to five fields that matter, plus the identifier. **Not the column dump.**
> - **Relationship** — 1:1, 1:N, M:N, with the M:N join made explicit. *(Say “one” and “many,” not “crow's feet.” CS 240's own ERD example is a class diagram with foreign-key arrows, so crow's-foot notation was never actually taught to them, and the worked example labels its lines 1 and N instead. Don't name a notation the target doesn't use.)*
> - **MVP vs. roadmap** — solid border vs. dashed.
>
> **Re-aim — three things that are new even though the notation isn't:**
> 1. **Key fields, not columns.** Types, nullability and defaults are exactly the over-specification failure they diagnosed in Monday's sample (planted defect #4). A dumped column table on the whiteboard is a teaching moment, not a mistake to ignore.
> 2. **The dashed border is a scope instrument, not a modeling one.** It's the MVP argument from CP3, showing up in the data model. A team with more dashed entities than solid ones has a scope problem, and this is the cheapest place all semester to find it.
> 3. **If they chose a document store or DynamoDB** — plausible, after CS 340 — the entities and relationships are still the first pass. Model what the system remembers and how it relates; access patterns and partition keys come second; the *choice* of store belongs in Round 5, not here.
>
> **The cross-check, which is the real point of the round:** walk the Round 3 trace and confirm (a) every step that reads or writes lands on an entity, and (b) every entity is touched by something. Orphan entities are roadmap features that snuck in; missing entities are the flow's un-owned state from Round 2, now visible twice.
>
> **Common errors and the correction:**
> - **Column dumps** — see above.
> - **Modeling the UI.** "SearchResult" and "DashboardView" are screens, not entities.
> - **Missing the join.** M:N stated as a line with no join entity; they know how to fix this, they just skipped it.
> - **The kitchen-sink model** — fourteen entities for a five-week build. Dash everything the core flow doesn't touch, then count what's left.
>
> **If a team is stuck, say:**
> - *"What does the user see a list of? That's usually an entity."*
> - *"What does the system have to remember between two visits?"*
> - *"Which of these does your core flow never touch? Dash it."*
>
> **What "done" looks like:** five to eight boxes, key fields only, relationships labeled, roadmap entities dashed, and every trace step landing somewhere.

### 6. Round 5 — decisions & alternatives — 10 min

- Each team takes its **two biggest decisions** (from Monday's sketch — stack choice, hosting, data store, real-time vs. polling, whatever they are) and writes, for each: *the decision, one real alternative, and why the choice wins given our goals.* Three sentences each — this is the alternatives-considered section drafting itself.
- Instructor spot-checks: is the alternative *real* (something a reasonable team might pick) or a straw man?

> **Instructor notes — §6 (Round 5: decisions & alternatives)**
>
> **Status: new, and the one with the highest grade weight behind it.** They have rich trade-off *vocabulary* — CS 240 compared N-tier, microservices, P2P, event-driven, serverless and the actor model; CS 340 compared patterns, SQL vs. NoSQL, and cloud services. What neither course asked is for them to **choose for their own system and defend it in writing.** This round is where that starts.
>
> **The block to put on the slide** — it is the template's §6, verbatim, so drafting is transcription:
> - **Chosen:** …
> - **Alternative considered:** …
> - **Why the chosen option wins, given our goals:** …
> - **Evidence:** spike / benchmark / prior experience / *"none yet"*
> - **What would change our mind:** …
>
> **Teach — the three-word clause that does the work: *given our goals*.** A decision defended with generic virtues ("it's faster," "it scales," "it's modern") is an assertion. A decision defended against *their* goals and non-goals is engineering. **At course scale the criteria that are actually real are:** what the team already knows, what a five-week build can absorb, what the user's real environment requires (offline? a phone in a basement? a shared laptop?), and what a future contributor can set up without your help. **Scale is almost never a real criterion here** — a team invoking horizontal scaling for forty users is reciting CS 240, not deciding. Say that gently and it lands.
>
> **On evidence:** this is where the CP3 spike pays off, and Monday's sample showed both ends — the photo-downscaling decision argued from a real measurement, and the Postgres decision honestly labeled "reasoned, not measured." **Both are acceptable; only the unlabeled one isn't.** "None yet" is a fine entry and better than a bluff.
>
> **On "what would change our mind":** ten seconds to write, and it's the line that separates a decision from a commitment. It also seeds the risks table (§7) and the retrospective question in CP11 ("which decisions held?").
>
> **Which decisions to pick** — if a team dithers: *"Name the choice you'd be most annoyed to have to redo in November."* The usual candidates: language/stack, data store, hosting and deployment, authentication, web vs. mobile vs. CLI, real-time vs. polling, single-tenant vs. multi-tenant, one repo vs. several.
>
> **Common errors and the correction:**
> - **The straw man.** "We chose X over writing our own from scratch." Not a real alternative. Ask what a competent team down the hall would have picked.
> - **Scaling cosplay** — see above.
> - **A non-decision dressed as one.** "We chose Python because we all know Python" is a *legitimate* decision at this scale — but then say so plainly, name the real alternative, and be honest in "what would change our mind." The problem is never the humble reason; it's the invented grand one.
> - **Two decisions that are really one.** "Django" and "Postgres" often collapse into one stack choice. Push for a second, genuinely independent decision.
> - **Deciding in the room for the first time.** If the team is actually arguing rather than documenting, that's a good use of the ten minutes — but make them write the block before the bell, even if the "chosen" line ends up as a §7 open question with an owner and a date.
>
> **If a team is stuck, say:**
> - *"Finish this sentence: 'A reasonable team might have picked ___ instead, and here's why we didn't.'"*
> - *"What would have to be true for you to switch?"*
> - *"Is that reason about your users, your team, or the five weeks? If it's none of the three, it's not a reason."*
>
> **What "done" looks like:** two filled blocks, each with a named alternative a reasonable person would pick, a reason tied to their goals, and an honest evidence line.
