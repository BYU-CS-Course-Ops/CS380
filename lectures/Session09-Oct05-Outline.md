## Purpose of this session

Wednesday, everything changes: you pitch your project to the room, the room evaluates, and the ideas that recruit become the **anchor projects teams will be built around** (announced the following Monday, after everyone has read the proposals). Today is the full dress preparation — the craft of a persuasive technical pitch (structure, slides, delivery, Q&A), the **evaluation rubric** everyone will score with (you need to know how you'll be judged *and* how to judge), and rehearsal in small groups. A pitch here succeeds at two things at once: it **defends the idea** (real need, feasible scope) and it **recruits** — it makes a classmate think *"I want to spend my semester building that."*

---

## Learning objectives

By the end of this session, a student can:

1. **Structure a 6–8 minute pitch** that argues need → solution → feasibility → ask.
2. **Design slides that help** — few, visual, big-font (the 75-inch-screen standard applies to them too).
3. **Pitch to recruit** — make the call-to-join explicit and credible.
4. **Handle questions honestly** — including "I don't know yet, and here's how I'd find out."
5. **Evaluate a pitch with the shared rubric** — consistently, independently per criterion, and **as an outside observer**: answering "would you join?" as though they had no project of their own.

## Timed outline (≈75 min)

### 1. Framing — what Wednesday decides — 6 min
- The hinge: pitches → peer scores + recruiting interest → **anchor ideas announced Wednesday** → a weekend with the written proposals → **teams announced Mon, Oct 12.** Individual work ends this week.
- **Name the split and why it exists:** you evaluate live, because a pitch is a live event and the room's reaction is real signal — but you *commit* after reading the proposals, because scope, stack, and richness are better judged from a document than from six spoken minutes. It's the same divergence-then-deliberate-convergence move the course has taught since Week 1, applied to the biggest decision of the semester.
- The two jobs of the pitch: **defend** (the idea holds up to scrutiny) and **recruit** (people want in). The rubric weights persuasion/recruiting highest — audience effect is part of the grade.
- Reassure: the solo path stays open; a pitch that doesn't recruit a team can still proceed at solo scope.

### 2. The pitch arc — 11 min
- A 6–8 minute skeleton (adapted from Kawasaki's ten slides down to our scale):
  1. **The problem & who has it** (~90 sec) — lead with the user and the evidence, not the app.
  2. **The solution** (~90 sec) — what you'd build; the vertical slice, shown concretely.
  3. **Why it's feasible** (~2 min) — scope, stack, top risk + mitigation; the honest-confidence move from CP3.
  4. **Why it's worth joining** (~90 sec) — richness: what a teammate would get to build (data? UI? infra?); where it grows.
  5. **The ask** (~30 sec) — explicit: *"I'm looking for 1–3 teammates; here's what we'd do first."*
- The most common failure: spending five minutes on the problem and thirty seconds on feasibility. Time-box per section and rehearse against the clock.

### 3. Slides that help (not hurt) — 8 min
- **The floor, in one line:** ~5–7 slides · nothing under ~30pt · one idea per slide · no slide you would need to read aloud. *(Kawasaki's 10/20/30, scaled to our block.)*
- Then **five contrasts**, each shown as a pair, each teaching one move. These are the deck's do/don't slides:
  1. **The workaround vs. the bullet list.** A photograph of what users actually do today — the spiral notebook and the shoebox of printed photos — against a slide reading "• Hard to find items • Time-consuming • Error-prone." The photo *is* the argument; the bullets summarize an argument the audience never saw.
  2. **The number vs. the adjective.** "3 of the 4 wardrobe leads I interviewed keep inventory on paper" against "many users struggle with this." An adjective is what you write when you don't have the number, and a room full of engineers hears the difference.
  3. **One diagram vs. the logo collage.** A single boxes-and-arrows sketch of the vertical slice — phone → search → catalog → photo store — against a wall of framework logos. A diagram makes a claim you can question; logos are decoration that invites "why that stack?" with nothing on screen to answer it.
  4. **The ask vs. the thank-you slide.** A closing slide reading *"Looking for 1–3 teammates. First thing we'd build: the photo-tagging flow"* against "Questions?" in 60-point type. That last slide stays up through the entire Q&A — spend it on the thing you actually want.
  5. **The one-idea slide vs. the agenda slide.** A six-minute talk does not need a table of contents; cutting it buys back thirty seconds for the problem.
- **The principle underneath:** slides are evidence and memory aids; **you** are the pitch. If the deck could deliver itself, it's a document — and you already submitted the document.
- *Instructor note — where contrasts 1–3 come from:* they are the classroom form of Michael Alley's **assertion–evidence** approach (listed in today's optional readings) — a **sentence assertion + visual evidence** in place of the phrase headline + bullet list, with comprehension research behind it. **Credit it out loud in one sentence**, both because students should hear that this is a documented finding rather than the instructor's taste, and because citing the source of a borrowed method is the behavior this course keeps asking of them.

### 4. Q&A under pressure — 8 min
- **Which rubric — name it:** this is **CP4's *Handling of questions*, 15 points of 100, scored by the instructor** (`rubrics.md`; they read the assignment for today, so point at it rather than re-explaining it). It rewards composure and honesty, not omniscience. *(Peers score the neighboring behavior as **Preparedness** on their sheet — "knows the idea's weaknesses, answered honestly." Two instruments, one behavior; the full walkthrough is §5.)*
- The moves: answer the question asked (not the one you rehearsed); *"I don't know yet — here's how I'd find out"* is a strong answer; never bluff a feasibility question, the room can tell.
- Likely questions to prep for: *Why would anyone use this over X? What's your riskiest assumption? What gets cut first if you're behind at Week 10?*

### 5. Two instruments — how you'll be graded, and how you'll score — 11 min

**Put both on screen together.** The most common Pitch Day confusion is students believing their classmates grade them. They don't.

| | **CP4 rubric** | **Pitch Evaluation Sheet** |
|---|---|---|
| Who fills it in | The instructor | You, for every pitch but your own |
| What it measures | Your **pitch** — clarity, persuasion & recruiting (25, the heaviest criterion), feasibility defense, handling of questions, slides, delivery | The **idea** — the seven selection criteria, plus "would you join?" and two comment lines |
| What it decides | Your CP4 grade (8% of the course) | Input to **selection and team formation**; the instructor holds final say |

- Say the asymmetry plainly: **peer scores inform the instructor's judgment; peers do not assign grades.** That is what makes honest scoring safe — and honest scoring is itself professionalism: the same evaluation move they ran on their own ideas in Week 3, and the one they'll run on design docs, PRs, and funding decisions for the rest of their careers.
- **Then calibrate on one criterion, out loud.** Use **scope realism**, and use the costume-catalog project the room dissected in Session 7 — so nobody is learning to score while scoring their own idea:
  - **3 — strong.** *"v1 is one theater's wardrobe: photograph and tag what's already in the shop, search it from a phone on the shop floor. No borrowing between theaters until that works. About six real build hours a week for five weeks — roughly thirty hours."* Bounded, arithmetic shown, and you can picture the demo.
  - **2 — adequate.** *"The MVP is the catalog, search, and borrow requests — and I'll get the inter-theater sharing working if there's time."* The core is plausible; "if there's time" is where a second, unbounded project hides. Score the sentence, not the intent.
  - **1 — weak.** *"A platform where every community theater in the state shares costumes — accounts, messaging, and recommendations."* The 10 × 14 = 140 arithmetic in a nicer shirt.
- **Two rules that prevent most bad scoring**, said before Wednesday: score each criterion **independently** — a pitch can be a 3 on value and a 1 on scope realism, and that combination is the most useful signal selection gets — and score **against the criteria, not against the previous pitch**.
- **Calibrate the recruiting question too.** "Yes" means you would genuinely consider spending your semester on it — a signal, not a compliment. "No" alongside high scores is perfectly consistent: a strong idea that isn't for you. And the sheet now asks for **one line of *why***, which is worth more to the presenter than the checkbox — the difference between "nobody picked me" and "two people said the domain didn't grab them, but the project was sound."
- **Then name the bias that will otherwise ruin this data — spend a full minute here.** Every evaluator in this room is also a presenter, so the reflex answer to "would you join?" is *"no, I have my own project."* **That is not information.** Everyone has their own project; if the whole room answers that way, the sheet tells the selection nothing, and every presenter learns only that their classmates were busy.
  - **The posture to teach:** answer as an **outside observer with no skin in the game** — *"if I walked in today with no idea of my own, looking for something worth a semester, would this be it?"* That is the question the checkbox is actually asking.
  - **Say why it is safe to answer honestly.** Checking Yes commits you to nothing: team formation happens after the instructor blends the input, and finding someone else's project compelling does not forfeit your own. An honest Yes costs nothing; a defensive No makes a good project read as unwanted.
  - **The tell, in the *why* line:** a reason about **you** ("I already have my own idea," "I'm committed elsewhere") is the bias talking. A reason about the **project** — the domain doesn't interest me, I don't believe the scope, I'd have nothing to build there — is information the presenter can use.
  - *Same discipline as reviewing a PR whose approach competes with your own, or judging a proposal that would take budget from your team: evaluate the thing on its merits, then declare your interest separately.*

### 6. Build it, then run it once — 25 min

*Students arrive with a proposal, not a pitch. Rehearsing before there is anything to rehearse produces a nervous read-aloud of CP3 — so the block is split: quiet build time first, then one honest run.*

**6a — Build time (solo, quiet) — 10 min.** On the **Pitch Build Sheet** (`resources/Pitch-Build-Sheet.pdf` — half-sheet, one per student), not in slide software. Say it explicitly: **you are not making slides right now; you are deciding what the pitch says.** The sheet has four boxes:
1. **The five beats, each with a time budget** — problem & who has it (~90s) · solution, shown concretely (~90s) · why it's feasible (~2 min) · why it's worth joining (~90s) · the ask (~30s). Writing the budget next to the beat is the only real defense against the five-minute problem statement.
2. **One piece of evidence for beat 1** — the specific number, quote, or observation from discovery you will say out loud. Not the evidence base; the one line.
3. **The ask, written verbatim** — word for word, so it doesn't evaporate at minute six. *"I'm looking for 1–3 teammates; the first thing we'd build together is ____."*
4. **A slide list — titles only, 5–7 of them.** What each slide is *for*, no content yet. Building slides is tonight's job; deciding which slides exist is today's.

Circulate and read over shoulders. Two things to catch: a beat-1 evidence line that is still an adjective, and a slide list that is really the table of contents of the written proposal.

**6b — One practice round — 14 min.** Pairs (one triad if enrollment is odd), mixed domains so the listener is genuinely cold.
- **Deliver beats 1–3 only** — problem, solution, the opening of feasibility — **standing, from notes, ~3 min against a visible timer.** Not the whole pitch; the front of it, which is where pitches are won or lost.
- **The listener does three things (~2 min):** scores just **value** and **scope realism** on a practice half-sheet, asks **one** question from the §4 list, and gives one line in the rubric's language.
- **Swap.** Two runs of ~5 min, ~4 min for transitions and reset.
- *Why only two criteria today:* the point is to hear the register and feel the clock, not to run a full scoring pass. The whole sheet gets used Wednesday.

Circulate, note the two or three failures the room shares, and name them in the wrap — that debrief is worth more than a third rotation would be.

### 7. Wrap + Wednesday logistics — 6 min
- **Announce the mechanics; leave nothing ambiguous:** **the order is drawn at random and shown at the start of class** — so *everyone* arrives ready to go first — **6–8 min + 2–3 min Q&A**, a visible timer, **paper evaluation sheets** at the door (one per pitch you watch), and **slides posted or loaded before class starts.** No day-of fiddling with dongles and downloads.
- **Say the consequence of a random order out loud:** there is no back-of-the-room strategy and no watching two pitches to see how it's done. Rehearsing tonight is the only preparation that works, which is the point.
- **Say the team-size policy out loud so recruiting asks are calibrated:** teams are **~2–4**, and **continuing solo is a fully supported path** — not a consolation prize — at a scope sized for one person. With a small class, several projects are likely to run solo. Ask for what you actually want.
- **Tonight:** rehearse **out loud, against a timer, twice.** Cut until it fits. *Then* build the 5–7 slides you listed today — in that order, because slides built before the words are decided end up being the words.
